The Anatomy of Phishing

How a single click brought down governments, corporations, and massive networks.

TARGET: 2016 US PRESIDENTIAL ELECTION

The John Podesta Bitly Hack

In March 2016, John Podesta, the chairman of Hillary Clinton's presidential campaign, received an alarming email. It appeared to be an official security alert from Google, warning him that someone in Ukraine had his password and that he needed to change it immediately.

The email contained a "Change Password" button. However, the link didn't go to Google. It went to a Bitly shortened URL (a common phishing tactic) which redirected to a fake Google login page controlled by Russian state-sponsored hackers (Fancy Bear).

An IT staffer reviewed the email and made a fatal typo, replying that the email was "legitimate" instead of "illegitimate." Podesta clicked the link, entered his credentials, and gave hackers access to over 60,000 private emails.

The Impact: The stolen emails were released via WikiLeaks in the weeks leading up to the election, causing immense political damage and dominating the news cycle. A single malicious link shifted the course of global politics.
TARGET: RSA SECURITY (2011)

The "2011 Recruitment Plan" Excel File

RSA Security was one of the premier cybersecurity companies in the world, responsible for the SecurID two-factor authentication tokens used by defense contractors, banks, and the military.

Attackers sent two targeted spear-phishing emails to small groups of RSA employees over a period of two days. The email subject line was simply "2011 Recruitment Plan," and attached to it was an Excel spreadsheet.

When an employee opened the spreadsheet, it executed a zero-day vulnerability in Adobe Flash (which was embedded in the Excel file). The malware installed a backdoor (Poison Ivy RAT), giving the attackers a foothold inside RSA's corporate network.

The Impact: Hackers stole the "seed values" for the SecurID tokens. Months later, those stolen seeds were used to bypass two-factor authentication and hack into U.S. defense contractor Lockheed Martin. RSA had to spend $66 million to replace 40 million hardware tokens for customers worldwide.
TARGET: UBIQUITI NETWORKS (2015)

The $46 Million Executive Impersonation

In 2015, Ubiquiti Networks, an American technology company, fell victim to a highly sophisticated "Business Email Compromise" (BEC) phishing attack. This didn't involve malware or malicious links—it relied purely on social engineering.

Attackers researched the company's hierarchy and impersonated the CEO and a prominent lawyer. They sent highly targeted emails to the company's finance department in Hong Kong, claiming there was a strictly confidential corporate acquisition underway.

The emails directed the finance employees to wire millions of dollars to overseas bank accounts to fund the "secret acquisition." Because the emails looked like they came directly from the CEO (using a slightly altered lookalike domain), the employees complied.

The Impact: By the time the fraud was discovered, Ubiquiti had wired $46.7 million to accounts in Russia, Hungary, China, and Poland. They were only able to recover about $8 million.
TARGET: SONY PICTURES ENTERTAINMENT (2014)

The Fake Apple ID Verification

Before releasing the devastating wiper malware that erased Sony's servers and leaked unreleased movies, the "Guardians of Peace" (later attributed to North Korea) needed a way inside the network.

They spent months targeting top Sony executives (including the CEO, Michael Lynton) with sophisticated spear-phishing emails. Many of these emails appeared to be from Apple, asking the executives to "verify their Apple ID" by clicking a link.

The link took them to a fake Apple login page. Because many executives used the same passwords for their personal Apple IDs and their corporate Sony accounts, the hackers easily gathered the credentials needed to access Sony's internal systems.

The Impact: Complete network destruction. Attackers leaked executive salaries, embarrassing internal emails, unreleased blockbuster films, and personal data of 47,000 employees. The attack cost Sony an estimated $100 million.
